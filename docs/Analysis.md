### 1. Why shouldn't consultants use free Gemini or ChatGPT?

Your Huber needle example is a fair test, but it addresses only the initial classification question. We have never planned to charge consultants simply to determine a device's basic regulatory pathway. That capability is free in our business model.

The commercial opportunity is the substantial work that follows, particularly predicate research, regulatory evidence collection, documentation and preparation of defensible deliverables.

Our market analysis already recognizes free AI combined with openFDA as a direct competitor. We must therefore demonstrate a measurable productivity advantage rather than simply claim that we are AI-powered.

Here is where MedexRegAI is designed to differentiate itself:

- **Predicate research:** Search the FDA's 510(k) database, rank candidate predicates and generate side-by-side comparison tables with traceable FDA summaries, including checks for relevant recalls. Our PRD targets at least 60% less time for this work.
- **Regulatory research:** Assemble relevant FDA summaries, guidance, recognised standards and recall information into a project library, using updated source data and identifying superseded information where supported.
- **Traceability and reproducibility:** Preserve dated source records, project history and action logs so consultants can reproduce their research and explain how a conclusion was reached.
- **Evidence-backed outputs:** Require regulatory claims to trace to actual source records, flag unsupported conclusions and distinguish verified facts from AI-generated analysis.
- **Consultant workflow:** Provide separate client and project workspaces, reusable research, document exports, gap analysis, FDA correspondence triage and submission history.
- **Confidentiality and accountability:** Design for client-data separation and appropriate data protection, with a proposed determination guarantee subject to clearly defined terms and validation.

These capabilities are intended to help consultants defend their work to clients and support regulatory review, rather than merely obtain an answer from a chatbot. We should not suggest that any software can guarantee FDA acceptance or eliminate the need for expert judgment.

I agree with your underlying point: an experienced consultant may be able to reproduce some of these results manually using free AI and FDA resources. Our business case therefore depends on proving that MedexRegAI saves enough time and effort to justify its price.

**For Friday's demonstration, I suggest we use the same Huber needle example and compare Gemini directly against MedexRegAI.** We should request the five best candidate predicates, their K-numbers, recall status and a side-by-side comparison supported by FDA source documents. We can then independently verify the results against the FDA database and measure the time, accuracy, completeness and manual corrections required.

The objective is not to prove that Gemini is incapable of answering the question. It is to demonstrate whether MedexRegAI can produce a more complete, traceable and reviewable deliverable with substantially less consultant effort.

Our business plan already includes a week-12 decision gate: at least 50% less time spent on predicate research and regulatory research in a live 510(k) workflow. If we fail to meet that threshold, we should not proceed with the larger build without reconsidering the product.

### 2. US Agents versus the actual consulting market

I agree that a US Agent and a regulatory consultancy are not equivalent. The FDA's US Agent data is useful for identifying prospects, but it does not represent a complete census of consulting businesses.

The 1,201 establishments acting for two or more manufacturers may include agents and distributors that do not provide the consulting services we intend to support.

For planning purposes, I propose using approximately 340–650 established consulting firms as a provisional working range, subject to validation. At our assumed annual subscription of $18,000, that represents an illustrative annual software revenue opportunity of approximately $6.1 million–$11.7 million.

There is also a potential gap in the other direction. Consultants who prepare 510(k) submissions exclusively for US manufacturers may not appear in the US Agent data. The FDA's 510(k) records identify correspondents, which may provide an additional route to identifying these firms, although we need to investigate the available data and deduplicate the results.

We should distinguish the verified prospect list from the estimated total market and avoid presenting either as a definitive census until the methodology is validated.

### 3. Small consultancies and one-time purchases

I agree that small consultancies are unlikely to be the first customers for an $18,000 annual subscription.

Our current model already allows free seats and free basic pathway determination. The proposed $500 project price is intended to cover the broader regulatory workflow, with the consultant potentially billing the project back to their client.

We could also test a limited, self-service report for approximately $99, such as a predicate shortlist, with no onboarding or training. Our current estimated delivery cost of approximately $35 per project suggests this may be commercially feasible, subject to confirming the actual support, infrastructure and review costs.

I would not prioritize building a separate low-cost product yet. Instead, we should test this price during consultant interviews and focus our initial pilot on established firms with recurring project volumes and a stronger economic incentive to subscribe.
